03 Introduction / Rationale

I teach ICT to Senior High School (SHS) students in Grade 11 and Grade 12. In the first quarter I introduced CodeQuest, a gamified, project-based approach in which lessons are organized into short “missions” (hands-on computer tasks) that earn points and badges and lead to a small digital project that students build and present.
Many students could follow my demonstrations but struggled to apply the skills on their own. Participation dropped during long lecture-and-demo sessions, learners with limited computer access at home fell behind, and written tests did not show whether students could actually perform the tasks.
ICT competencies are performance-based, so students need frequent hands-on practice, immediate feedback, and real-world tasks. A gamified, project-based approach gives every lesson a clear goal, raises motivation, and lets me assess skills through working outputs instead of memorization.

04 Summary of the Innovation Implemented

CodeQuest divides each unit into missions. Each mission has a short mini-lesson, a hands-on task done in pairs or small teams, and a quick digital check (Quizizz or Google Forms). Completed missions earn points and badges on a class leaderboard, and a final unit project (a website, mini program, or app prototype) is scored with a rubric. Lessons, instructions, and submissions are managed in Google Classroom so students can keep working outside class.
• Increase participation and motivation in ICT lessons through game elements. • Develop hands-on skills through short, practical missions. • Assess competencies through working outputs, rubrics, and digital quizzes. • Strengthen collaboration and troubleshooting among learners.
Students will be able to apply basic computer, coding, and web-design skills to complete a working digital output; troubleshoot common errors with teammates; collaborate responsibly on a shared project; and use digital tools safely and ethically.
Before, I relied on teacher demonstration, an individual activity, and a written test. CodeQuest changes the lesson flow: students start with a mission, get immediate feedback, earn recognition for progress and teamwork (not only final scores), and show mastery through projects. Digital checks also reveal learning gaps during the lesson, not after it.

07 Instructional Materials / Tools Developed

• CodeQuest mission cards and point/badge tracker • Lesson presentations and short instructional videos • Hands-on worksheets and coding / web-design exercises (Python, HTML/CSS/JavaScript) • Google Classroom posts and assignments • Quizizz / Google Forms quizzes for digital checks • Project rubrics and a peer-evaluation form • Class leaderboard and badges (printed and digital)
Mission cards gave students clear, step-by-step tasks and kept lessons moving. Presentations and videos introduced concepts briefly, then students practiced right away. Quizzes showed me who needed reteaching during the lesson, rubrics made grading transparent, and Google Classroom let absent or slower students catch up and resubmit work.

12 Results and Impact

The gamified, project-based approach produced working outputs from every team and gave me clear evidence of skill mastery through rubrics and quizzes. See the assessment tally, student outputs, and feedback sections for the actual figures.
Students moved from copying demonstrations to building and fixing their own outputs, showing stronger application of coding and web-design skills. (Support this with your tally and rubric scores.)
Participation, teamwork, and willingness to present improved, and more students submitted tasks on time through Google Classroom.
The evidence suggests CodeQuest is an effective strategy for hands-on ICT lessons. Limitations: results come from one class and one quarter, device access affected pacing, and more cycles are needed to confirm the gains.

13 Reflection of the Teacher

Short, hands-on missions with immediate feedback keep students more engaged than long demonstrations. I also learned that clear rubrics and team roles prevent confusion and unequal participation.
Mission cards, pair programming, the team leaderboard, and quick digital checks that showed learning gaps early.
Pacing when computers or internet were limited, and balancing competition so it rewards teamwork and improvement, not only speed. I also need better ways to track each student's contribution in team tasks.
I now plan lessons around what students will do, not what I will explain. I use data from quick checks to reteach right away, and I design assessments around real outputs.

15 Conclusion

CodeQuest showed that gamified, project-based learning can make ICT lessons more active, collaborative, and meaningful. Through missions, quick digital checks, and rubric-based projects, students practiced real skills and I could see their learning clearly. Despite limited devices and mixed skill levels, the strategy worked with pair work and tiered tasks. Next, I will refine the missions, strengthen individual accountability, and share the approach with fellow ICT teachers.
